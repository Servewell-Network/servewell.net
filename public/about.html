About

ServeWell.Net exists to help people serve well. It begins with Scripture, through which we discover our identity, clarify our purpose, and move toward healthy relationships.

ServeWell.Net is for a mixed audience and is growing quickly. For the most current view of what is available, the Features page highlights the latest content and functionality.

ServeWell.Net is short for ServeWell Network. Think of that as a network of people that help each other. This is not intended to be one of those websites where you see problems but have no way to offer something better. Please offer improvements and suggestions either on GitHub or increasingly through the features of ServeWell.Net itself, and let us know if you're willing to take credit for the changes or if you prefer anonymity. As spam increases, you may need to work harder to prove you're a real contributor of content, but don't give up! Only together can we experience God's good plan for our world.

Manuscript Types

When you tap a Bible word, the popover typically shows a Source field describing which manuscript traditions contain that word or reading. ServeWell.Net groups manuscripts into three categories:

Early manuscripts
The oldest surviving witnesses to the biblical text. For the Old Testament this includes the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation). For the New Testament it includes the Alexandrian manuscripts (such as Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus) and the critical editions that draw on them (such as the Nestle-Aland text).
Common manuscripts
The widespread received tradition — the text form found in the majority of surviving copies. For the Old Testament this is the Masoretic Text, the authoritative Hebrew Bible preserved by Jewish scribes. For the New Testament this is the Byzantine or Majority Text, the form found in most Greek manuscripts and often in the King James Version.
Outlier manuscripts
Readings found in manuscripts that fall outside the two main streams above — for example, certain lectionaries, some church father quotations, or small minority text families. These readings are noted where they affect the wording but do not fit neatly into either the early or common traditions.

No single category is presented as more authoritative. The labels describe the age and distribution of the sources, not their theological weight.
